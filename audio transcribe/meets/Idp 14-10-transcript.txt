## Main Discussion
The faculty member emphasized that the student project must connect to an **implemented or formally sanctioned real-world project**, rather than remain a classroom-style technical exercise. Students should engage with stakeholders such as clients, contractors, and site engineers; visit the project site; understand its practical constraints; and make a concrete contribution. A site visit that amounts only to observing or briefly visiting the location would not meet expectations. The project should offer interdisciplinary exposure—for example, a water-resources project may also involve geotechnical, structural, or transportation considerations.

## Potential Project Direction
The students proposed fine-tuning one foundation model and then applying it to a real project, rather than training several models in isolation. The faculty member agreed this could be a reasonable direction if it is tied to an actual project. She suggested exploring participation in Professor Vasudev’s long-running Mahanadi Basin work, including access to the site, discussions with engineers, and identification of a practical problem. She offered to inform Vasudev about the guidelines, while stressing that arranging the opportunity is the students’ responsibility.

## Examples of Successful Teams
The faculty member described three highly placed teams from the previous year:
- **First place:** A team working with Sandhanchana, a construction repair and rehabilitation company, evaluated new column-wrapping methods. Students visited the implementation site, prepared and tested wrapped samples in the laboratory, and conducted SEM analysis to assess repair and structural performance.
- **Another leading team:** A team working with L&T on the Mumbai Coastal Road Project examined areas including traffic, geotechnical, and structural work, with students dividing the project across these disciplines.
- **Third place:** A construction-management team implemented camera-based tools at a construction site to document worker efficiency and identify shortcomings.

These examples illustrated that strong projects combined real industry or site access with practical investigation, measurement, analysis, and contribution.

## Scope, Timeline, and Expectations
The students noted that their industrial mentor had asked them to complete work involving roughly five or six foundation-model training tasks by the 17th and would be unavailable afterward. The faculty member questioned the purpose of that workload and advised them not to prioritize producing many outputs over meeting the project’s core expectations. She acknowledged that the remaining time was limited—approximately two to three weeks after exams—and urged the students to discuss the constraints with their mentors and revise the scope if needed.

The final presentation is likely to be evaluated on specific evidence of site engagement: what the team did there, what information or measurements it obtained, and how those findings shaped subsequent work. The team should identify a real problem, address at least some aspect of it, and refine its problem statement accordingly. The faculty member offered help if challenges arise.
